# Refined DAMPING Estimation FOR MACHINING structures 

## Abstract

Experimental Modal Analysis (EMA) has played a crucial role since the late 1940s. Today, many engineers consider the field to be a closed case, with mature commercial software providing standard solutions across various industries. However, a close inspection of the underlying algorithms reveals that significant room for improvement remains in many relevant cases, particularly in systems with nonproportional damping. 

General structures in machining exhibit non-proportional damping, rendering the standard assumption of proportional damping untenable. Accurately modelling these characteristics is vital for modern manufacturing applications, such as chatter suppression utilizing tuned mass dampers or stability analysis in multi-cutter systems. 

Currently, mainstream software for EMA is proprietary, which limits accessibility and obscures specific algorithmic implementations. To benefit the engineering and research community, we introduce Indar, an open-source Python package for EMA. Among other advanced capabilities, Indar implements robust algorithms tailored specifically for identifying non-proportional damping systems. 

## Introduction

The finish quality of a workpiece is affected by multiple factors, such as the dynamic response of the machining structure to the forces involved during manufacturing. Therefore, it is important to model the structure and operating forces to mitigate the effect of vibrations, and to avoid unstable conditions that could give rise to chatter [Muñoa 2016]. 

One of the main frameworks for the modelling of vibrating structures is Experimental Modal Analysis (EMA), which relies on the assumption that the dynamic is linear. A standard mechanical model, based on viscous dissipation, is 

Mx′′ + Cx′ + Kx = f, (1)

where x ∈ ℝ𝑛 is the displacement from equilibrium, n are the Degrees of Freedom (DoF), and M, C, and K are the mass, damping, and stiffness matrices, which are symmetric and positive definite [Ewins 2000]. 

Mature software for EMA includes Simcenter™ Testlab™ and BK Connect®, and many consider them the ultimate solutions. However, despite the good performance of these programs, there are still relevant situations where there is room for improvement over the current algorithmic approaches. This work focuses on one of these particular situations: damping estimation. 

Much of the theoretical foundation of EMA lies on the assumption that there exists a coordinate basis that decouples the mechanical system (1) into n single DoF oscillators; these systems are known as proportionally damped systems. However, these systems constitute a negligible fraction of all possible mechanical systems, and this assumption could severely misrepresent a vibrating structure, which in turn could imply wrong conclusions about its stability. Hence, this work is concerned with nonproportionally damped systems. 

Fortunately, if two modes with natural (complex) frequencies 
𝜆j ≠ 𝜆i, where 𝜆 =𝜔𝜁 + i1−𝜁2 , satisfy the relationship 


∣∣𝜔𝑖−𝜔𝑗∣∣𝜔𝑖 ≪ 𝜁𝑖, (2) 


where the symbol ≪ reads “much smaller than”, then the error introduced by ignoring the coupling between both modes is negligible [Hasselman 1976]. Notice, however, that this relationship is violated when the modes are close to each other, and the damping is sufficiently large. An interesting analysis and physical reinterpretation of these systems was proposed in [Baldelomar 2026]. 

In any case, the conditions under which significant coupling between modes occurs are not rare in practice. This motivated, for example, the study and modelling of machine tools with nonproportional damping in [Neugebauer 2010]. 

In [Reith 2017], during the analysis of a multi-cutter turning structure, the authors noticed a significant coupling between modes due to the large damping introduced by the fixturing. After comparing the stability lobes generated by models with coupled and decoupled modes, the authors concluded that nonproportional damping better reflected the properties of the stability lobes. 

Other systems prone to exhibit significant coupling between modes are Tuned Mass Dampers (TMD) because of the proximity between the modes. Recently, slender structures modelling tall buildings with TMDs located at some points were studied in [Bellos 2025], showing a noticeable difference when the couplings are ignored. 

In the context of machining, mechanisms based on TMDs are used for the suppression of chatter [Muñoa 2015]. However, commercial software for EMA is not equipped with methods to fit nonproportional damping, which forces researchers to use inappropriate techniques for this task such as peak picking [Hintze 2023] or surrogate models. To the best of our knowledge, the only program that truly tries to fit nonproportional damping is the Structural Dynamics Toolbox (SDT) [Balmes 2019]. 

As part of a more ambitious revision of EMA, we developed the open-source Python package Indar (this word comes from the Basque word for Force) [Indar 2026]. Among other innovations, this package offers methods to fit nonproportional damping to experimental data; see [Ponce-Vanegas 2026] for a more comprehensive description of capabilities. However, due to the very challenging nature of these systems, Indar does not offer a universal solution yet, so continued research is still needed to cover a broader variety of systems.
